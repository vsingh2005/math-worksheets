\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=customteal!20]
    \large\textcolor{black}{\textbf{Foundation: Algebraic Methods}}
\end{tcolorbox}

\vspace{3mm}

\noindent
Graphing is a great visual tool, but it's not always precise, especially when the solution involves fractions or decimals. We have two algebraic methods to find the exact intersection point $(x, y)$.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Substitution Method:} Best used when one variable is already isolated (e.g., $y = 3x - 2$). You substitute the expression for that variable into the other equation.
    \item \textbf{Elimination Method:} Best used when both equations are in standard form ($Ax + By = C$). You add or subtract the equations to eliminate one of the variables entirely.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{4mm}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=customteal!20]
\large\textcolor{black}{\textbf{Substitution Method}}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!25, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=4mm, left=6mm, right=6mm, top=5mm, bottom=5mm]

\textbf{Worked Example 1: Solving by Substitution}

\medskip

\textbf{Problem:} Solve the system:
\begin{align*}
    y &= 2x + 1 \\
    3x + 2y &= 16
\end{align*}

\medskip

\textbf{Solution:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Step 1:} The first equation tells us exactly what $y$ is equal to: $(2x + 1)$.
    \item \textbf{Step 2:} Substitute $(2x + 1)$ in place of $y$ in the second equation:
    \[ 3x + 2(2x + 1) = 16 \]
    \item \textbf{Step 3:} Solve for $x$:
    \begin{align*}
        3x + 4x + 2 &= 16 \\
        7x + 2 &= 16 \\
        7x &= 14 \implies x = 2
    \end{align*}
    \item \textbf{Step 4:} Plug $x=2$ back into either original equation to find $y$:
    \[ y = 2(2) + 1 = 5 \]
    \item \textbf{Answer:} The solution is $(2, 5)$.
\end{itemize}

\end{tcolorbox}

\vspace{4mm}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=customteal!20]
\large\textcolor{black}{\textbf{Elimination Method}}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!25, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=4mm, left=6mm, right=6mm, top=5mm, bottom=5mm]

\textbf{Worked Example 2: Solving by Elimination}

\medskip

\textbf{Problem:} Solve the system:
\begin{align*}
    2x + 3y &= 11 \\
    5x - 3y &= 10
\end{align*}

\medskip

\textbf{Solution:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Step 1:} Notice the $+3y$ and $-3y$. If we add the equations together vertically, the $y$ terms will cancel out (eliminate).
    \item \textbf{Step 2:} Add the equations:
    \begin{align*}
        (2x + 5x) + (3y - 3y) &= 11 + 10 \\
        7x &= 21
    \end{align*}
    \item \textbf{Step 3:} Solve for $x$: $x = 3$.
    \item \textbf{Step 4:} Plug $x=3$ into the first equation to find $y$:
    \begin{align*}
        2(3) + 3y &= 11 \\
        6 + 3y &= 11 \\
        3y &= 5 \implies y = \frac{5}{3}
    \end{align*}
    \item \textbf{Answer:} The solution is $(3, \frac{5}{3})$. (Notice how hard this would be to find by graphing!)
\end{itemize}

\end{tcolorbox}
